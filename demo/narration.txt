[1 · 0:00 · Teams meeting]

A normal daily stand-up. Three engineers on a call, one shared codebase. Listen to Dev: he is going to build a new feature on top of an existing piece of the code. Nobody on that call knows that a ticket already sitting in the backlog is about to remove exactly that piece.

[2 · 0:20 · transcript flies to the app]

The meeting ends, and the transcript goes straight to the first agent. It reads what everyone said, keeps the real commitments, and ignores the small talk and the admin.

[3 · 0:28 · Agent 1 drafts]

Two commitments become two draft tickets in Jira, clearly marked as drafts. That is all this agent is allowed to do: write drafts. It never touches anything else.

[4 · 0:42 · the code graph]

A new ticket wakes the second agent. It does not read the whole backlog. It follows each ticket into the code: which files it touches, and what those files depend on. Dev's feature leads down one path. The older ticket leads down another. The two paths meet on a single point in the code. That point is the conflict.

[5 · 0:58 · verdict and comment]

Both tickets turn red, and a comment appears on Dev's ticket: the exact sentence from each ticket, the place in the code where they collide, and a suggested order: build the feature first, or keep the old piece in place until the feature no longer needs it. Then the agent waits.

[6 · 1:10 · approval, then auto mode]

One label from a human, and the agent links the two tickets. In auto mode there is no waiting at all. Here a ticket that removes a login helper meets another that still relies on it. The agent comments, labels, and links, in one pass. Those are its only three actions.

[7 · 1:35 · the whole backlog]

Across the whole backlog, two hundred and six tickets, it caught four of the six conflicts we had hidden in there, with three false alarms. Everything happens inside Jira, in comments, labels, and links, right where the team already works.
